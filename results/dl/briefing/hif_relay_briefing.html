IEEE 13-bus digital-twin HIF protection · journal extension · progress brief, 27 Oct 2026

Wavelet features or an end-to-end CNN for the lateral HIF relay?

Random Forest (deployed relay), LightGBM and a 1D-CNN on raw residual windows, scored on the same 900 Emanuel-arc runs, the same run-grouped folds and the same 180 held-out test runs. Stages 0–4 are complete, including closed-loop fault clearing in the Simulink twin with 3- and 5-cycle breakers, an inter-relay interlock, and a noise-augmented retraining of all three models.

Bottom line

  • On clean signals, end-to-end learning does not justify replacing the wavelet front end. The best CNN detects 78.6 ± 8.5 % of test faults with 9 false trips, against 89.3 % / 4 for the deployed RF and 97.1 ± 1.0 % / 2 for LightGBM on the same 87 db4 features.
  • The receptive field is a real cause, but only part of the gap. The original CNN sees 63 samples (0.19 cycle). Widening it to 703 samples (2.1 cycles) at the same size lifts CV fault detection from 49 % to 70 %; the trees still sit at 92–96 %.
  • Under measurement noise the order flips, and noise-aware training widens the gap. Trained on clean data, the wide CNN holds about 79 % detection up to σ = 0.1 A per phase while the RF falls to 47 % and LightGBM to 62 %. Retrained with noise, all three improve, but the CNN most: 91 % detection and 92 % weak-arc detection at 0.1 A, still 89 % at 0.2 A, against 87 % / 64 % for LightGBM and 83 % / 50 % for the RF at 0.1 A.
  • Noise-aware training also lifts the CNN on clean signals, from 78.6 % to 90.6 % fault detection (weak arcs 90.5 %), level with the deployed RF and still below clean-trained LightGBM's 97.1 %. The trees pay a clean-signal price instead: LightGBM drops to 93.2 %, weak arcs 78.6 %.
  • In closed loop the breaker, not the classifier, sets the clearing time. With a 3-cycle breaker all three relays clear a typical arc in a median of 80–84 ms (about 5 cycles); the relay choice moves the median by at most 4 ms. The CNN is the fastest and steadiest (every trip within 33.6 ms), but without coordination it disconnects the healthy 675 lateral in 46 of 48 runs with a fault on lateral 633.
  • An inter-relay interlock fixes selectivity for every model. The first relay to trip blocks its neighbour; same-tick ties go to the lateral with the larger zero-sequence residual. Healthy-lateral trips fall from 64 to 0 across all 288 fault runs, with no change to any faulted-lateral trip time and no false trips on the decoys.
  • The CNN's clean-signal gap is a data-size effect. On a learning curve the trees level off by about 360 training runs, while the CNN is still climbing steeply at 720 (fault detection 43 → 79 %). A power-law fit puts LightGBM-level detection at roughly 13,000 runs, 18 times the present set. The noisy copies, which triple the windows, landed exactly where that fit predicts (90.6 % against 89.9 %).
  • The noise-trained relays keep the closed-loop guarantees. With the interlock and a 3-cycle breaker none of them trips the healthy lateral. LightGBM and the CNN clear all 32 fault runs (median 77 and 81 ms); the noise-trained RF misses the weak 675C arc in all four runs.
  • Recommendation for the edge relay: let the measurement noise decide, and keep the interlock either way. Train every model with noise: clean-trained LightGBM is best only with almost no noise, and its false trips jump from 2 to about 26 at just 5 mA. If the residual noise stays at or below about 20 mA (70 dB against the 633 lateral load), noise-trained LightGBM is the better relay (92–93 % detection, 2–3 false trips, every closed-loop fault cleared). At about 50 mA the two are not significantly different overall, with the CNN ahead on weak arcs. From 0.1 A up, the noise-trained wide CNN is the more dependable relay. So end-to-end learning does not replace the wavelet front end outright; it earns its place where the signal is noisy. A hybrid of the two is the natural next step.

How the comparison was run

Every model sees the same 25,803 two-cycle windows (667 samples at 20 kHz, three phases of the lateral-head residual Iphysical − Itwin). Folds and the test split are grouped by simulation run, so overlapping windows of one run never sit on both sides. All metrics come from one shared scoring function, which reproduces the deployed relay's stored numbers exactly.

Random Forest (deployed)

100 bagged trees on 87 db4 5-level features (sub-band energy, entropy, std, max, time-domain moments, zero-sequence terms). The in-model relay since Review 2.

LightGBM

Gradient-boosted trees on the same 87 features. Early stopping on 20 % of training runs; 212–285 boosting rounds.

1D-CNN, four variants

Raw 3 × 667 window, no wavelets. Base (26 k params, 63-sample view) and wide (31 k params, 703-sample view), each with signed-log or linear reference scaling. Label-safe time-shift augmentation.

Fault detection is the relay's 2-of-3 vote over each faulted lateral's windows (open loop). False trips count run/lateral sequences where the vote fires without a fault on that lateral, out of 360 test sequences. CNN and LightGBM rows are the mean ± sd of three training seeds.

Receptive-field ablation (cross-validation, 720 training runs)

The CNN taken from the hardware check covers 63 samples, about 3 ms. That is too short to compare the arc's positive and negative half-cycles, which is the Emanuel model's signature asymmetry and which the wavelet mean and skewness features capture directly. The ablation changes only the receptive field, at near-equal parameter count, in both input scalings.

Behaviour under measurement noise

White noise of fixed standard deviation σ is added per phase to the held-out test windows, exactly as in the Review 2 noise benchmark (same seeds; the RF reproduces its stored numbers to 4 × 10−16). The twin is noise-free, so the noise reaches the residual one to one. No model saw noise during training.

Random Forest (deployed) LightGBM (3 seeds) CNN wide, reference (3 seeds)
  • The trees lose dependability from about 20 mA. Their weak-arc detection collapses first: at σ = 50 mA it is 18 % (RF) and 32 % (LightGBM), against 82 % for the wide CNN.
  • LightGBM is the most noise-sensitive model on security. Its false trips rise from 2 to about 26 at σ = 5 mA, an 82 dB signal-to-noise ratio against the 633 lateral load.
  • The wide CNN is flat up to σ = 0.1 A and breaks down between 0.2 and 0.5 A, with about 47 and 161 false trips. A likely reason, not yet tested, is that the trees lean on the high-frequency D1–D2 wavelet bands that white noise fills first.

Noise-augmented retraining

All three models were retrained on the same set: the clean training windows plus two noisy copies of each (41,284 copies), each copy with its own σ drawn log-uniformly from 5 mA to 0.1 A. The training noise uses its own random stream and continuous σ, so no copy repeats a test level or test noise draw, and the sweep below still runs to 0.5 A. Recipes, hyperparameters and run-grouped early stopping are unchanged; LightGBM and the CNN again use three seeds.

  • Do the trees regain the lead once they expect noise? Only on clean signals. Noise-aware RF and LightGBM hold up to 50 mA, but weak-arc detection falls at 0.1 A (50 % and 64 %) and collapses at 0.2 A. The noise-trained CNN stays flat to 0.2 A, twice the largest noise it was trained on, and only breaks down at 0.5 A (59 % detection).
  • The CNN's clean-signal gain is the most telling result. The extra noisy copies triple its training set, and its clean fault detection rises 12 points to 90.6 %, with seed spread shrinking from ± 8.5 to ± 2.0 points. The learning curve below confirms that 720 training runs are too few for end-to-end learning.
  • Security stays acceptable. The noise-trained CNN makes 5–6 false trips per 360 sequences up to 0.2 A. Up to 0.1 A every one is a sympathetic vote on the neighbouring lateral, the kind the interlock removed in closed loop; at 0.2 A one seed adds one vote before the fault starts. The noise-trained trees make 2–6 up to 0.1 A, but from 0.1 A some of theirs fall on normal or switching runs, and LightGBM reaches 16 at 0.2 A.

Learning curve: is the CNN short of data?

Each model was trained on nested subsets of 25, 50, 75 and 100 % of the 720 training runs (stratified by event type and dataset, three repeats with different subsets and seeds, same recipes) and tested on the same 180 held-out runs.

  • The trees have what they need; the CNN does not. RF and LightGBM gain little beyond 360 runs, because the db4 features already encode the physics. The CNN is still climbing at 720 runs, and its spread across repeats broadly shrinks as data grows.
  • How much more data? A power law fitted to the CNN's missed-fault rate (exponent −0.68) reaches LightGBM's 97.1 % at roughly 13,000 training runs, 18 times the current set. This is a long extrapolation from four noisy points; treat it as an order of magnitude.
  • The same fit explains the noise-augmentation gain. It predicts 89.9 % detection for three times the data; the two noisy copies per window, which triple the training windows without adding runs, gave 90.6 %. Noise augmentation works for the CNN largely as data multiplication.
  • So the clean-signal gap is a data-size effect, and closing it by simulation alone would take an order of magnitude more runs. The wavelet front end is, in effect, prior knowledge that a CNN would otherwise have to learn from data.

The table's CNN and LightGBM classifier times come from their compiled libraries (PyTorch, LightGBM C++), so read across environments with care. By operation count the wavelet route is the lighter one: a 5-level db4 decomposition of three 667-sample phases plus the statistics is on the order of 0.1 M arithmetic operations by our estimate, against about 2 M multiply-accumulates for either CNN. On a relay with compiled firmware, the wavelet + tree pipeline should be the cheaper of the two; in interpreted MATLAB the CNN is five times faster.

Closed-loop fault clearing in the twin (Stage 4) measured

Each relay runs inside ieee13_hif_twin_system.slx, and its trip opens the lateral breaker in both the feeder and the twin. 300 runs: three relays × breaker operating time 0, 3 or 5 cycles × the 8 HIF verification cases × 4 inception instants spread over one 8.35 ms decision period, plus the healthy feeder and three switching decoys per relay. The MATLAB LightGBM and CNN reproduce the Python labels on all 5,161 test windows, and with zero breaker delay the edited model reproduces the original 12-case suite sample for sample.

t₂

Trip asserted. The 2-of-3 vote latches.

t₃

Inter-relay interlock built and verified

A coordinator between the two relays and their breaker commands. The first relay to trip is granted; its neighbour is blocked for the breaker operating time plus 60 ms (its two-cycle window, the slowest arc extinction and one decision period). A blocked relay is reset every step, so once the block lifts it can only trip on fresh evidence of a fault on its own lateral. When both relays latch on the same 8.35 ms tick, the lateral with the larger zero-sequence residual energy over the last two cycles wins. With the interlock switched off, the model reproduces the original 12-case suite sample for sample.

  • Median clearing times are unchanged (at most one 50 µs sample, because the healthy lateral now stays energised). The decoy cases still cause no trips.
  • The tie-break has to use the zero-sequence residual. The first version broke ties on total residual energy and picked the healthy lateral in 6 of 12 ties: a weak arc carries less current than the neighbouring lateral's response to the voltage sag, so the faulted lateral then waited out the whole block and cleared 75–158 ms later. Measured at the tie instant, only the zero-sequence residual was larger on the faulted lateral in every tie (worst ratio 1.42). That fits the physics: an HIF is a ground fault and returns as 3I0, while the sag response of the healthy lateral is largely balanced.
  • The 12 ties are only 4 distinct situations, because the breaker time cannot change anything before the trip, so the zero-sequence rule is well founded but lightly validated.

Open items and caveats

  • The noise model is white Gaussian noise on the residual only, trained up to 0.1 A. CT saturation, harmonics from the measurement chain and noise on the twin side are not modelled.
  • The closed-loop twin runs without measurement noise, so it checks the noise-trained relays' timing and selectivity, not their noise tolerance.
  • The learning-curve extrapolation (about 13,000 runs to reach LightGBM) rests on four noisy points and should be read as an order of magnitude.
  • Results come from one simulated feeder with one arc model; there is no field data yet.
  • The breaker is a fixed operating time followed by the Simscape breaker's current-zero interruption: no arc inside the breaker, no restrike and no reclosing.
  • The interlock assumes one fault at a time: a genuine second fault on the neighbouring lateral within the block window (breaker time + 60 ms) is held until the window ends. Its tie-break rests on 4 distinct tie situations.
